# S258 — Model and code comparison for game bug localization

Isis Roca, África Domingo, Óscar Pastor, Carlos Cetina and Lorena Arcega, *Comparing MDD and CcD in the Bug Localization Context: An Empirical Evaluation in Video Games*, CAiSE 2024, LNCS 14663, pp. 581–595, [DOI 10.1007/978-3-031-61057-8_34](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-031-61057-8_34). Main-agent reconstruction, 2026-10-04. **Complete selected author edition:** all 16 pages of text and page visuals, two figures, two tables, model equation and 21 references; pages 4, 10 and 11 enlarged. This is a 16-page author preprint, not a verified copy of the 15-page publisher chapter.

The study compares locating error points in two supplied Kromaia scenarios, represented as UML object diagrams or C++ construction code. Its 44 students and ten professionals produce no significant overall method contrast in performance or productivity; professionals have higher code scores, while satisfaction ratings favor models. The released numeric data reproduce the descriptive results. This is consequential counterevidence to transferring a favorable model-based feature-implementation result directly to bug localization. It does not establish successful repair, an isolated representation effect, or a general disadvantage of model-based development.

## What participants receive and do

The commercial game supplies the domain and underlying SDML modeling approach. The paper describes SDML models interpreted into C++ runtime objects; that capability is background, not the observed experimental activity. Here the model condition supplies a static scenario diagram plus metamodel, while code supplies scenario construction code and eight class headers. Both receive a bug report, game image, training/example material and instructor assistance. Participants write lists of suspected locations in a form: model object/attribute paths or code line/object/attribute descriptions. They do not submit a repaired, compiled or behaviorally validated game.

The shared solved example concerns excessive damage and zero reload time. Participants are told each scenario has between three and ten error points; the answer key contains six. Task 1 concerns a boss stuck during movement: two arm positions, two link endpoints and two mobility properties. Task 2 concerns an apparently unkillable boss: two occluding object positions and mass/material properties of two vital hulls. These are several parameter/configuration faults associated with one reported symptom per scenario. Six ground-truth locations are not six independently sampled gameplay defects.

The paper reports 58 people performing tasks, of whom 54 submit the form and consent to data use; the four omitted outcomes/reasons are not reconstructed. Two other people participated in a pilot. The analyzed sample is 44 third-year object-oriented-programming students and ten professional game developers, recruited by convenience. Students attend the first session in person and professionals the second through Teams. Experience, cohort and session setting therefore cannot be separated by this contrast. The published schedule is approximately 1h45 with instruction, a video, form/setup time, two nominally 30-minute tasks and a focus group. Forms promise neither payment nor academic credit and describe the focus group as unrecorded notes. The Spanish training deck itself totals 100 minutes; schedule and actual individual recorded duration are separate records.

Assignment counterbalances **method sequence**, with group A using model→code and group B code→model. **Task/scenario order is fixed:** everyone encounters task 1 before task 2. The analysis dataset has 20 students/5 professionals in A and 24 students/5 professionals in B. A sequence effect may involve preparation, carryover or task interaction; a period effect is also a task effect. Neither is an isolated test of learning. The domain is shared with S255/S256, but participant overlap across those publications is not established and must not be assumed absent.

The released representations differ beyond surface notation. For example, task 2's main-hull life is 200 in the diagram and 2000 in code; a bottom-hull link targets the bottom hull in the diagram but repeats the top-hull target in code; left-vital y position is −1 in the diagram and +1 in code. Task 1's tail mobility values also differ. The solution's code entry for one task-1 link names `topArm`, while the supplied faulty line targets `leftArm`, matching the model's listed fault. These are source/answer-key discrepancies, not demonstrated runtime outcomes. They prevent treating the released materials as a verified semantically equivalent notation control. They do not justify replacing the recorded scores or erasing the observed contrast.

## Outcomes and reconstruction

Performance is **F1 for listed locations**, combining precision and recall against the answer key; productivity is F1 per self-reported minute. It is not the fraction of fully repaired bugs or passing behaviors. The paper describes 223 possible locations in task 1 and 200 in task 2, with six positive locations each. The reported potential-location universes are not reconstructed as executable oracles. Satisfaction uses 16 five-point questionnaire items aggregated into perceived ease of use (PEOU), usefulness (PU) and intention to use (ITU).

## Reported inference and its limits

The primary paper compares four repeated-subject mixed models with method, experience and their interaction, optionally adding sequence and period; it selects models using residual normality and smallest AIC/BIC. Table 2 selects model 3 for performance/productivity and model 1 for satisfaction. Overall method p-values are **.350/.369**, while method×experience p-values are **.041/.025**. PEOU and PU method p-values are **<.001**, and ITU is **.008**. The primary claims significant professional performance/productivity differences, but gives no exact subgroup p-values. Student significance occurs only under its simplest model; the released model including group/task gives **.210/.109**. These results support a conditional, activity-specific account rather than a universal method ranking.

The released statistical exports impose concrete qualifications. The aggregate seven-page PDF exports only two ITU fits, not the complete set promised by the README. It shows a **nonconvergence warning** for the fit with method, experience and interaction. Its ITU method F=7.620/p=.008 matches the paper; the experience F is **7.592**, whereas the paper repeats 7.620 in that column. The student adjusted-performance export warns of a non-positive-definite Hessian, and the adjusted-productivity export warns of nonconvergence. The simplest student's method-only syntax omits the intercept and prints a two-degree-of-freedom method test; that output alone does not verify the one-degree-of-freedom model-versus-code contrast implied by the prose. These are source-located inferential limitations, not a new fitted analysis.

The professional PDF's actual fit on pages 1–3 is ITU with group/task (method p=.150), followed by diagnostic/effect-size material. It does not provide the claimed professional performance/productivity tests in readable PDF form. The corresponding acquired SPV has additional binary tables, which remain unparsed; therefore those subgroup p-values and the full aggregate AIC/BIC comparison are **not independently verified**. A passive supported conversion of those outputs, or a fuller author export, is the executable evidence follow-up if the precise inferential claim becomes decisive. No claim of successful statistical reproduction follows from matching descriptive means.

Small professional n, convenience selection, session/cohort confounding, fixed task order, unverified representation equivalence and source-limited inference bound transfer. The paper's reported interaction and adverse professional means remain in the survey. These limitations do not turn its favorable model satisfaction or inconclusive pooled outcomes into evidence of absence.

## Qualitative evidence and implications for Nu

The released focus notes are more specific than the paper's general claim that professionals prefer code. On the initial easier/more-intuitive question, **six of ten professionals favor models, two code and two neither**. On the final best-material question, the split is **three models, two code and five indifferent**. Both favor neither a uniform code preference nor the equation of preference with performance. Most students prefer models. The notes report that some model-first participants found the subsequent code easier, but this is an account of their experience, not randomized mediation evidence.

The practical obstacles are consequential: unfamiliar domain rules, constructor-argument lookup across headers, understanding spatial configurations and uncertainty about whether a suspicious value is erroneous. Instructors clarify mobility and vital points; one professional cannot see SVG attributes until advised to use a browser. Some students use the metamodel during the code task; the supporting-material use is not a strict model-information exclusion. Comments request execution/video feedback, describe difficulty without seeing the faulty behavior, and mention that an IDE could expose constructor attributes. The paper's call-navigation explanation and unconnected-model-element explanation remain hypotheses; the study does not randomize navigation tools or directly demonstrate either mechanism.

For **B10**, retain this study alongside [S257's integrated Unreal implementation comparison](S257-unreal-model-code-comparison.md), [S255](S255-game-product-lines-comparison-partial.md) and [S256](S256-game-product-line-predecessor.md): task, environment, feedback and outcome definition change across them. For **B01/B03/B12**, distinguish seeing labeled properties, identifying a location, performing a change and satisfying semantic/temporal obligations. A runtime interpreter or visual representation does not itself demonstrate correct diagnosis, repair or migration. Conversely, lower model localization scores do not refute model-based implementation gains in a different task.

For the Nu survey, the useful research question is what representation, source context, navigation and observable behavior make a particular evolution obligation tractable, under explicit costs and comparators. Nu's pinned source and practitioner claims still require their own mechanism-to-obligation evidence; this study supplies neither an F#/C# comparison nor a diagnostic-enumeration experiment.
