## Comparative method and reported results

Sections4.1–4.3, pp4694–4696:12paid convenience-sampled participants, mostly undergraduate/graduate students, with some recruitment from professionals but no separate professional subgroup result. Ages21–35 (mean24.6), Unity experience2–10years (mean5.3), visual-scripting experience mean2.1years; two had NodeCanvas experience and two had studied BTs. An initial16-person target was reduced to12 during COVID. Remote machines vary and four use a second monitor.

The12participants form **six pairs**. Each performs three tasks in one supplied RPG template, beginning from empty BTs with player movement and three helper functions already implemented. Tutorials and warm-up precede actual tasks. Table3 crosses six between-subject mode orders with three within-subject modes. Table4 enumerates all six permutations across pairs: Slime is task1, TurtleShell task2, Beholder task3; each mode occurs with each character for two pairs. Pairing modes and tasks is randomized without replacement. Fixed character order remains distinct from balanced mode order.

In **None**, participants work offline/alone with vanilla NodeCanvas and cannot communicate or coordinate. In **Async/Sync**, they work together with NCCollab and can choose whether to use auxiliary features. Thus staffing/communication and a feature bundle change together. Outcomes are CSI, its weighted factors, an author-developed seven-point questionnaire, logged completion times and post-study interviews. There is no isolated preview/history treatment, long-term maintenance task, external correctness score or learning test in this reported comparison. The timing origin/stopping rule, individual-versus-pair logging treatment and dyad dependence are not reconstructed from raw logs; the reported ANOVAs use participant-level degrees of freedom without an explicit pair model. Six pairs are not12independent teams.

Simple arithmetic from reported time means yields reductions of **5.07minutes (23.78%)** for Async and **3.75minutes (17.59%)** for Sync relative to None. These are elapsed-time comparisons, not person-minute savings or adjusted causal estimates. Preserve the practical positive result. The authors themselves attribute faster work to sharing the task; unknown total labor does not negate that shorter completion time. Nonsignificant mode/order tests neither establish equivalence nor prove all order effects absent.

The authors likewise explain (§5) that collaboration is the likely driver of total CSI differences. In Table5 None's collaboration factor is zero; the other factor tests are null. A larger total therefore does not establish a general creativity gain or learning improvement. Fig23 also preserves nonuniform ratings: perceived efficiency is favorable for67% in None,58% in Async and50% in Sync, without a significant reported difference. Overall ratings favor the package more often (33%,75%,58%, respectively). Keep those distinct rather than describing every measure as improved.

## Use, adverse observations and transfer

Interviews (§4.5–§5) favor remote awareness/live preview; eight participants prefer Async and four Sync. Quick prototyping/game jams are suggested uses, while educational/mentor scenarios are proposed rather than independently evaluated. History is useful to some participants but skipped by others on small fixed tasks. The paper's exact usage count is inconsistent: §4.5.2 identifies P3,P5 and P12 as non-users, whereas §5 says10/12used it. Do not silently select one count.

Reported difficulties include confusion between preview and working canvases, desire for ownership/locking indicators, missed silent messages, missing text wrap/Enter-to-send, and requests for voice chat. Eight participants use conflict resolution and four use visual differences **after task completion** to compare their final BTs. That ordering matters: the reported completion time does not clearly disclose whether this final reconciliation is included. Future-use suggestions, observed use and measured completion time remain different outcomes.

The study supplies a useful small task comparison of **collaborative visual authoring versus solitary visual authoring**. It does not compare visual versus textual languages, F# versus C#, Nu architectures, pure versus mutable state, or complete lifecycle costs. Generalization to exclusive professional teams, large/open-ended projects and persistent runtime migration remains unestablished. Transfer to other BT editors is an author proposal, not a tested cross-engine implementation.
