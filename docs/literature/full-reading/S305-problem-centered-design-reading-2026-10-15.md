## What was designed and what was evaluated

ChemTrains was intended to let people without programming experience construct animated graphical models with qualitative behavior, such as document flow, a Turing machine or a physical process. Objects move along visible or invisible paths and participate in reaction-like rules when they meet in places. The work began with Office and Bunsen Burner examples, then expanded through concrete difficulties rather than from a complete abstract requirements specification (pp. 5-8).

The report distinguishes three units:

- A **raw problem** is a system-independent account of something the intended system should support. The six design problems were Bunsen Burner, Office, Tic-Tac-Toe, Maze, Petri Net and Turing Machine (Table 1, p. 7).
- A **micro problem** isolates a difficulty exposed while applying an alternative to a raw problem. Examples include coordinating a Turing-machine head, modifying a memo without replacing its identity, sharing a Petri-net input place, controlling paint inside overlapping places and coordinating turn state across Tic-Tac-Toe places (pp. 8-16; Figures 1-2).
- A **scenario/walkthrough** is design-specific: it traces how a proposed programmer could reach a solution through decisions and system actions. Problems can compare designs; scenarios are bound to one design (pp. 2-3).

Problems both generated and rejected alternatives. They also caused scope revision. For example, the designers first treated generic distribution-list interpretation and memo modification as requirements of Office. Re-reading the raw requirement showed that a fixed demonstration sufficed, allowing simpler designs to remain in contention (p. 12). Conversely, Tic-Tac-Toe and Maze were added because one designer could not expose the limitations of the early system on the two original easy problems (pp. 8, 15-16). The record is therefore a design process in which tasks, alternatives and doctrine co-evolved, not a preregistered comparison of fixed systems.

The growing space was collapsed into three deliberately contrasting packages (pp. 16-18; Table 2):

| Design | Selection principle and key facilities | Consequential limits in the walkthroughs |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **ZeroTrains** | Chose simpler alternatives where possible; places, objects and paths have simple graphical forms; reactions use before/after specification and can exploit overlapping/nested places plus Clayton's Membrane Rule. | No explicit variables or absence test; rules operate on objects in one place; locations and absence must be reified as marker objects; cross-place conditions need enclosing places, catalysts or messenger objects. |
| **ShowTrains** | Chose concreteness and programming by demonstration; programmers manipulate a model and use highlighting to say which names, graphics and containment relations matter. | The user must decide object/place/path categories, names, graphics and highlighting; variables are limited; rule conflicts lack direct ordering. |
| **OpsTrains** | Chose power, influenced by OPS rule systems; structured objects and attributes, nesting, variables, rules over paths/places/objects and rule ordering. | Its advantages may depend on nontrivial representational choices, such as inventing markers that make groups describable; the walkthrough doctrine gives only general guidance for these choices (p. 28). |

## Method and actual evidence units

The evaluation had four linked phases.

1. **Expressiveness on six development problems.** The designers attempted a reasonable solution to every raw problem in every design. Last-minute design changes were allowed while these solutions were prepared; Clayton's Membrane Rule entered ZeroTrains during this stage. The report says reasonable solutions were found for all 18 design-problem combinations (p. 18). There is no independent correctness oracle or executed-program result.
2. **Doctrine construction.** Starting from those target solutions, the designers wrote guidance intended to connect problem features to programming actions. They repeatedly traced routes, revised inadequate doctrine, sometimes replaced clever or trial-and-error solutions with routes that were easier to explain, and tightened raw problem statements to specify a common solution approach (pp. 18-20). The resulting doctrine and target solutions were thus fitted jointly to the six development problems.
3. **Walkthroughs of record.** With design features and doctrine fixed across the six problems, the team wrote one walkthrough for every problem in every design. Each critical decision was linked to a problem feature and a doctrine item. The authors explicitly say this gave each design its best possible showing because the designs and doctrine had been developed against those problems (p. 20).
4. **Held-reserve walkthroughs.** Four larger problems - Doorbell, Grasshoppers, Sunspots and Copier - were withheld from design discussion and the first evaluation. The team then applied every fixed design and fixed doctrine to every reserve problem, for 12 additional analytical walkthroughs (pp. 29-31; Table 4).

The units are therefore **three authored design packages, ten authored problems, and 30 analytical design-problem walkthroughs**. The report does not identify human participants, participant recruitment, an allocation procedure, observed programmers, task completion data, or a sample-size rationale. It refers to hypothetical prospective users and to what a programmer would need to decide. The three named authors describe their design deliberations collectively; Victor Schoenberg contributed early discussion/prototyping and Robert Weaver helped develop the walkthrough approach, but the report does not supply analyst-by-walkthrough assignments or inter-analyst agreement. Those details must not be reconstructed from author count.

No evaluated design was implemented even as a prototype. The design-space figure records earlier prototype experience as one input, and the acknowledgments mention early prototyping, but the authors state that the facility comparison was conducted without implementing the three designs (p. 34). There are no participant times, errors, preferences, behavioral logs, implementation costs, uncertainty estimates or statistical tests.

## The Bunsen Burner worked comparison

The appendix makes the method unusually inspectable. The fixed problem asks for a manipulable high/low/off control, corresponding flame state, and ice/water/steam state (Table 3, p. 20). Each design receives written doctrine plus a step-by-step solution path.

OpsTrains maps the visible objects and containment structure directly into six replacement rules: three for the flame and three for the water. Its variables let a rule abstract over the previous flame or water graphic (pp. 21-24; Figure 4; Table A-2a). ShowTrains also needs six reaction rules, but the route includes choosing objects versus places, naming them, and highlighting exactly which name, graphic or containment aspects define each demonstrated rule (pp. 24-25; Table A-2b). ZeroTrains first writes events, then reifies control locations, replaces the absence of a flame with a `flameno` object, encloses cross-place conditions in a larger place, adds a catalyst and finally expands disjunctions because rules cannot use variables. It requires 24 reaction rules (p. 26; Tables A-1c and A-2c).

This example illustrates the report's central measurement distinction. A restriction can leave the final artifact only modestly larger yet impose considerable work in finding and translating a solution. Looking only at final representations would miss the marker, absence-rewriting and cross-place reasoning exposed by the walkthrough (p. 28).

## Findings, including adverse evidence

All three packages passed the authors' permissive expressiveness assessment on all six development problems. The facility assessment nevertheless ranked **OpsTrains first, ShowTrains second and ZeroTrains last** (p. 27). Doctrine-item counts were similar on their coarse definition - 21 OpsTrains, 24 ShowTrains and 26 ZeroTrains - although the paper says differences of form and content prevent precise comparison (pp. 19-20). The proposed simplicity benefit of ZeroTrains did not materialize: its single-place restriction and lack of absence tests repeatedly moved complexity into representation and workaround steps.

The favorable OpsTrains result was not simply that its final solutions used fewer rules. Its variables and rules over multiple represented elements let the doctrine connect stated problem relations to language operations more directly. The authors found it surprising that this power did not require substantially more doctrine and used a Query-by-Example-style route: construct a concrete rule, then replace selected constants with variables (p. 27).

The report also preserves reasons this ordering may be too favorable to OpsTrains:

- Grouping with variables can require prior marker and representation choices whose discovery the doctrine does not fully explain. Similar concerns apply to ShowTrains naming and graphics (p. 28).
- Doctrine was jointly refined with development tasks and their target solutions; the 18 initial walkthroughs are demonstrations under fitted guidance, not out-of-sample observations.
- The analysis concerns **shallow program construction** where doctrine is assumed sufficient to avoid much search. It omits understanding, testing and modification, and may not predict deep programming that requires representation search and backtracking. The authors explicitly allow that a limited design could fare better there (pp. 34-35).
- The three proposed designs were not implemented. Feasibility, actual interface cues, runtime behavior and user problem solving remain unobserved (p. 34).

The reserve set gives a useful but bounded adverse/transfer check. Fixed designs and doctrine produced the same broad ordering, with an even larger reported OpsTrains advantage because the problems were bigger and limited variablization required many more rules in the other designs (p. 29). Yet the reserve problems exposed requirements that the development set missed: direct treatment of time-dependent processes in Doorbell, Sunspots and Grasshoppers; richer structured state in Grasshoppers; movement of paths for the Doorbell circuit; and graphic rotation for the Copier belt. None of the designs handled time directly, and none supported the required rotation (pp. 29-31). The authors therefore treat reserve agreement as partial confirmation while conceding that the original set did not cover the intended design space.

## What this changes for Nu roles and learning

This report strengthens S307's use as a **qualitative design and preparation procedure**. It supplies the missing concrete distinction between evaluating a resulting representation and tracing the work needed to arrive there. For a selected Nu role/task, an analyst can record a system-independent requirement, the candidate's successive decisions, the knowledge or cue proposed to justify each decision, and workaround steps induced by a facility. Final scene size, syntax or rule count alone cannot establish ease of construction.

It also sharpens the status of the [R07/R20 preparation inventory](../../nu-learning-and-role-contract-2026-10-11.md). A package includes both facilities and doctrine. Documentation, worked examples, tool cues, specialist explanation and prior representation knowledge are therefore treatment inputs with costs, not neutral background. A design may have more mechanisms yet need less workaround doctrine when those mechanisms match the task. Conversely, reducing mechanisms can shift complexity into markers, coordination objects or external reasoning. These are testable mechanism predictions for an actual Nu task, not measured onboarding or productivity effects.

The raw/micro-problem split is useful for the existing role contracts. The system-independent R11/R12 behavior requirement should remain distinct from micro problems induced by a particular scene/reference or propagation design. A micro problem can justify an observation or a competing design without being promoted silently into the practical requirement. The Office reinterpretation is a warning that designers can accidentally demand a generic mechanism when the task only requires a bounded instance; the later addition of harder tasks is the converse warning against evaluating only examples that cannot expose a claimed advantage.

Reserve problems support a prospective transfer discipline: keep some consequential tasks outside construction and doctrine development, freeze the relevant package, then inspect whether guidance and mechanisms transfer. Their value here is qualitative. Four authored reserve cases do not establish representative coverage, and a later empirical Nu comparison still needs task provenance, common behavioral obligations, actual tool access, independently checked outcomes and all assigned costs.

This reading does **not** validate a readiness test, training threshold, learning score or human/agent benefit estimate. There were no observed users, no measured preparation, no independent task completion and no reliability assessment in this report. The completed S304 and S306 readings close the reported user-protocol and final-method access gaps, respectively, while still supplying no complete prediction denominator, independent coding reliability or validated readiness threshold. The R07/R20 contract should continue to treat a walkthrough as a hypothesis inventory to be challenged by actual choices, behavior, assistance and unexpected valid solutions. The report's own favorable ranking is evidence about the authors' analytic comparison of shallow construction under evolved doctrine, not about novices, trained users, maintainers, coding agents or Nu.
