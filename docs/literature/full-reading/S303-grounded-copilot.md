## What the study establishes

The paper develops an interpretive account of Copilot-assisted programming. In **acceleration**, people already have a next action and use suggestions to carry it out. In **exploration**, they use suggestions to help decide how to proceed. These are interaction modes within tasks, not fixed classes of people, a randomized intervention or measured acceleration relative to unaided work. The dual-process analogy is an explanatory framing, not a demonstrated cognitive mechanism.

Twenty recruited participants include fifteen from academia and five from industry; nine had previously used Copilot. Recruitment used contacts and social media, excluding people who had never used their task language. Participants were unpaid, with preview access supplied where needed. An approximately hour-long session included a five-minute familiarization task, roughly 20–40 minutes of core work, think-aloud observation and a semi-structured interview. Prior exposure, language familiarity and occupation are different variables (§3, Table 1).

The four task families were **designed and revised during theory development**, not sampled independently from professional maintenance. Chat-server work used supplied networking and a small state machine; chat-client work required unfamiliar networking/custom cryptography; Benford's Law mixed familiar sequence generation in Rust with unfamiliar plotting in Python; Advent of Code supplied a precise string-transformation task and example tests. Tasks and languages were not randomly crossed. A pair of illustrated users in §2 explains the modes; it is not two additional participants.

The first two authors coded the first two videos together, then one coded subsequent sessions and both discussed them. After eight sessions, emerging patterns informed further task design. They report saturation at participant twenty and later recode the videos using a closed codebook (§§3, 5). This makes the account systematic and auditable, but the same observations help generate and quantify it. No independent double-coded agreement statistic or timestamp-error assessment is reported. Five public livestreams, coded by one author, provide additional examples under different conditions; they do not establish independent annotation reliability or a representative replication.

## Useful findings, including contrary cases

The positive observations are concrete: suggestions can supply familiar small operations, unfamiliar syntax/API examples, an editable control structure and overlooked cases. People often accept part of a suggestion or combine pieces, and can use code effectively without reporting a deep understanding of its API. In a future Nu workflow, useful scaffolding and eventual understanding should therefore be measured separately.

Other observations qualify an automatic productivity interpretation. Large suggestions can interrupt an existing plan; generated code can be difficult to diagnose; participants may over-rely on the tool, accept then extensively rewrite, or spend time cleaning prompts out of source. A failed file-opening example is an observed difficulty, not a universal claim about AI debugging. Prompt comments sometimes express human planning and sometimes address the model, so their intent cannot be inferred from syntax alone.

For the user's convergence concern, §4.2.4 is especially relevant: some participants trusted recurring API patterns across suggestions or assumed the first suggestion was best, while others struggled to distinguish alternatives. These are reported/observed confidence and anchoring behaviors, **not evidence that repeated outputs are independent or correct**. Keep the source of each alternative and its substantive difference visible; a larger suggestion list does not establish broader requirement coverage.

The paper's broad meaning of validation is any action intended to increase confidence. It distinguishes examination, execution, static analysis and documentation (§4.2.5). Crucially, it **does not time static-analysis use** in the quantitative breakdown, because the checker runs in the background and actual use of its result is hard to identify (§5.1.3). Visible diagnostics, looking at them, a correct interpretation, running a check and satisfying the behavior are separate observations. This directly supports the existing R04/R05 invariant.

## Descriptive quantities and their boundaries

The paper reports 248.6 minutes coded as exploration and 104.7 as acceleration, with task-, familiarity- and prior-use-dependent patterns. These are coded interaction durations, not completion-time savings or all human work. Figure 3 normalizes each person's timeline; equal chart width does not mean equal exposure. Figures 4–5 stack separate mode medians, whose sum need not be the median of total time. No inferential comparison establishes a language effect, and Haskell/Java coverage is explicitly too small for such conclusions.

Figure 4a labels 9/2/11 expertise memberships. The released script resolves why they sum to 22: P12 and P13 contribute language-specific segments to different groups, while P11's two professional-language segments stay together. These are not 22 independent participants. The observed groups also differ in task and experience; they do not identify a causal expertise effect.

Figure 6's axis/caption describes time, whereas the prose refers to prompting occurrences and the released code counts coded events. The reported prompting percentages and duration summaries require the artifact qualifications below. The qualitative distinctions remain useful without treating these numbers as validated benchmarks or expected values for Nu.

## Consequence for future steering research

Use the modes as **sensitizing concepts**, with an unknown/ambiguous option and explicit evidence for an intent interpretation. Do not force every silent interval into one of two mental states or equate exploration with poor performance. Record what people ask, supply, select, edit, inspect and test; distinguish the intended goal, observable action, current artifact change and independently checked outcome. A human-supplied algorithm or translated code remains human contribution even when entered as a prompt.

This complements [S302's AI-permission/timing study](S302-metr-developer-productivity.md) and [S229's expert–AI case](S229-picoscenes-expert-ai.md). For Nu, a proposed visibility/representation advantage should help a specified decision or correct change after context, review and rescue work are counted. The source does not establish current-agent behavior, a Nu/F# effect, representative professional workflows, or an optimal steering policy. Its interface recommendations are proposals rather than evaluated improvements.

The method gap for drafting R21's observation vocabulary is now resolved. Reliable coding, behavioral validity, practical tasks, participant design and published numerical reconstruction remain future dependencies if their corresponding claims are pursued.
