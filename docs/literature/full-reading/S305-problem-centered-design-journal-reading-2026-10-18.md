## What remains stable from the report

The central design/evaluation structure survives. The authors use system-independent target problems to set scope and compare designs, and design-specific walkthroughs to trace how a proposed programmer could reach a solution. Microproblems expose difficulties or discriminate alternatives. The case still evaluates three authored packages - ZeroTrains, ShowTrains and OpsTrains - against six development problems, then freezes designs and doctrine for four reserved problems. This remains 30 analytical design-problem walkthroughs: 18 fitted development walkthroughs plus 12 reserve walkthroughs, not 30 observed programmers (pp. 321-326, 329-346).

The final edition retains the same broad ranking. OpsTrains is judged first for facility, ShowTrains second and ZeroTrains last. The doctrine remains similar in coarse size - 21 items for OpsTrains, 24 for ShowTrains and 26 for ZeroTrains - while the authors caution that differences in form and content defeat precise count comparison (p. 337). The Bunsen example still requires six OpsTrains rules, six ShowTrains rules and 24 ZeroTrains rules; the extra ZeroTrains work comes from representing absence and location and expanding cases that cannot be variabilized (pp. 339-343).

The reserve set again gives partial transfer evidence while exposing missed requirements. Doorbell, Grasshoppers and Sunspots require time-dependent processes absent from all three designs; Grasshoppers stresses structured state; Doorbell uses path movement not exercised earlier; and Copier requires graphic rotation that no design supports. The ranking survives, but the original task set did not cover the intended design space (pp. 344-346). The article also retains the limits to shallow program construction: it does not evaluate understanding, testing or modification, and a simpler system might behave differently when representation search and backtracking dominate (p. 352).

The important mechanism claim also survives. Final representation size alone can miss the work needed to discover and translate a solution. ZeroTrains' one-place restriction does not necessarily produce a much larger final solution, yet the walkthrough exposes substantial extra work to satisfy it (p. 343). This continues to support counting representation choices, doctrine and workaround decisions rather than treating artifact size as a facility measure.

## Material journal changes

### A prototype user check is added

The report edition contains no observed participant evaluation of the three proposed packages. The journal adds Section 2.13, explicitly describing it as an empirical check of the walkthrough result (pp. 346-348). The authors implemented a **minimal OpsTrains prototype** with an interactive graphics editor, pattern matcher and rule executor, sufficient for the original Bunsen-burner problem. This changes the final edition's empirical units: alongside the unchanged three packages, ten authored problems and 30 analytical walkthroughs, it now includes one implemented package, one original-set task and six individual users.

The six people were not six nonprogrammers: four had some traditional programming experience, one was described as an experienced programmer, and one had no programming experience (p. 346). They received doctrine essentially identical to the walkthrough doctrine, rewritten as sentences with jargon substitutions. Half read it; half had it read and explained. When a person stalled, the researchers intervened sufficiently to continue, directing them to relevant doctrine before adding new information (pp. 346-347). This is an assisted qualitative check, not a randomized training contrast or independent task-completion experiment.

All six applied straightforward doctrine and made initial progress; only the experienced programmer is reported as completing the whole task with minor problems (p. 347). All six struggled with the general execution model and failed to infer the need to draw objects that defined where reactions occurred, despite that point appearing in doctrine. The journal groups the misses into three mechanisms:

1. designers omitted basic facts because they were too close to the system;
2. plausible real-world expectations, such as spatial proximity, overrode the system's prescribed representation; and
3. people could sometimes identify where and how to use a variable yet refuse the action because they could not understand its consequence (pp. 347-348).

The third result directly qualifies the favorable analytic prediction on p. 343 that Query-by-Example-style doctrine would let users employ variables without much understanding. The journal concludes that informationally adequate instructions can fail when users do not understand their effects, and that empirical work may be necessary to reveal especially attractive false paths (pp. 347-348).

This is the **same ChemTrains user study** published in fuller form as [S304](S304-usability-testing-reading-2026-10-15.md), which the journal cites on pp. 346 and 353. S304 supplies details omitted from this summary: participant backgrounds, think-aloud video, direct versus experimenter-mediated operation, the four assistance fallbacks and before-fallback behavior counts. The journal adds no independent replication, participant cohort or second empirical task.

### The methodological conclusion becomes more adverse and better bounded

The final edition calls a programming walkthrough a partial substitute for, or preparation for, prototype evaluation and states that it is fallible (pp. 351-352). It still values the method for replacing unstructured intuition with an inspectable chain of decisions and knowledge, but the human check now shows that complete-looking doctrine can miss implicit system assumptions, compelling alternative models and users' need to understand consequences. A walkthrough is therefore a hypothesis-generating design analysis, not a validated predictor of readiness or completion.

This correction is material to the earlier report note. Statements there that no evaluated design was implemented and that there were no observed users remain accurate **for CU-CS-479-90**, but must not be generalized to the final journal edition or the whole publication lineage. The analytic three-design comparison itself remains unimplemented and has no between-design participant comparison; only OpsTrains receives the bounded prototype check.

### The journal abridges the reproducibility material

The report's 14-page Bunsen doctrine/walkthrough appendix is absent. The journal instead uses Figures 4-10 (pp. 337-342) for excerpts of the three doctrines, the Bunsen problem statement, two solution diagrams and two example OpsTrains rules, with prose summaries of the three walkthroughs. Across the full article it has 11 numbered figures and no numbered tables. The report therefore remains the fuller source for the complete Bunsen doctrine and step-by-step walkthrough materials, while the journal is the authoritative final source for the prototype check and revised methodological interpretation. CU-CS-480-90 remains a separate unread supplement for complete problem/case materials.

## Consequence for the Nu research agenda

The journal strengthens the existing R07/R20 preparation boundary in two directions. First, language mechanisms and doctrine remain a joint package: documentation, examples and explanations are treatment inputs and costs. Second, locating the prescribed operation, preferring it to a plausible alternative and understanding its relevant effect are distinct observations. A person or agent can repeat the correct instruction without being willing or able to act on it, while a rejected instruction may reveal either missing explanation or a mismatch between the representation and the practical task.

It does not validate a readiness score, training threshold, prediction-accuracy rate, agent measure or productivity benefit. The six users attempted one original development problem with assistance and changing instructional presentation; the journal gives no independent completion outcome for five, elapsed times, prediction denominator, coding reliability, raw protocols or follow-up cohort. The 30 walkthroughs remain designer analyses, and the S304 summary remains the same empirical unit. For a later Nu task, record exact access and assistance, the candidate's chosen representation, observed behavior and independently checked outcome; do not infer benefit from doctrine coverage or final artifact compactness alone.

**Disposition:** S305's final journal edition is now completely read. It confirms the report's analytic problem/doctrine/walkthrough method and favorable OpsTrains ranking, adds the already known S304 six-person prototype unit, materially weakens any claim that walkthrough doctrine predicts actual use, and removes the report's complete appendix. The remaining source gap is CU-CS-480-90 or underlying protocols/materials only when a selected claim requires them; it is no longer the journal body.
