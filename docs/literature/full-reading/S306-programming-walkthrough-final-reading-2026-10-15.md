## What the method actually asks analysts to do

The walkthrough analyzes a proposed route from a concrete problem to a program. Its distinctive output is not merely a solution or a list of language features. Analysts identify each proposed programming step and the **guiding knowledge** that could make a hypothetical intended user select it. That knowledge may concern the application domain, a problem-solving plan or a language feature. The analysts should not treat their own ease with a step as evidence that the intended user has the relevant knowledge (pp. 4-5, 9-12).

The final paper gives both a five-part outline and a seven-part cookbook (pp. 5, 10-12):

1. define the relevant language features;
2. choose realistic sample problems and outline intended solutions;
3. convene analysts, preferably including outsiders, while initially withholding intended solutions where useful;
4. work through the route, asking what happens next and what would justify that choice;
5. analyze long routes, choices without adequate guidance, extensive or esoteric prerequisites, unclear correctness, ambiguity and deviations from the designer's intended use;
6. compare alternatives when several designs or routes are relevant; and
7. revise the design or its definition, accept and support a valid unexpected route, or use the knowledge inventory to specify assumed background and documentation.

The final distinguishes **framing steps**, which select a plan or decomposition, from **application steps**, which carry out that plan with method knowledge. Each application step should have a prior or collapsed framing step; otherwise the analysis has probably skipped how a user would know to act (p. 11). This is a qualitative completeness check, not a scoring rule.

The paper asks three high-level questions: whether the route is unnecessarily long, whether any step lacks describable guidance and whether the required knowledge is unlikely, extensive or difficult (pp. 4-5). It supplies no calibrated step weights, proficiency threshold, inter-analyst agreement procedure, predictive-accuracy estimator or rule for aggregating these questions into a validated writability/readiness score.

Intended solutions receive special scrutiny because the method is meant to test a design rationale. They are not exclusive reference answers. Analysts may examine several routes, and the designer may accept a suitable alternative and change the design to support it. This supports retaining correct unexpected solutions in later work; it does not justify giving research candidates a successor solution or scoring only conformity to a preferred representation.

The cases support a qualitative claim that structured analysis produced useful design and documentation findings, including findings that surprised designers. They do not estimate a general treatment effect. The authors judge that every walkthrough repaid its cost, but provide no counterfactual cost comparison or measured return on investment. They report that MFD and CMPL had the best effort/result balance: no more than a day or two of analyst work each, including sessions, write-up and follow-up. They describe the more than 30 ChemTrains walkthroughs as possibly beyond what many designers would accept and DINO as too late for major design influence (pp. 23-24).

## Limits stated by the authors and limits of the evidence

Concrete problems make the reasoning inspectable but bound the result to the chosen tasks. The authors state that no finite set captures every relevant language demand; the simple initial ChemTrains tasks missed timing, synchronization and rotation. The walkthrough therefore supplements designer judgment and user testing rather than replacing either. Analyst judgment remains involved in identifying steps and deciding what counts as adequate guidance (p. 23).

The final article adds that walkthroughs can prepare user testing by removing known problems and focusing later observations. It does not show that analyst predictions are accurate enough to select users, certify readiness or replace observed behavior. Thinking aloud and walkthrough analysis answer different questions: users reveal difficulties they encounter, while analysts try to state every relevant step and its guidance. Reading both S304 and S306 resolves the reported protocol and final-method correspondence, but still does not yield prediction sensitivity, specificity, independent coding or a validated readiness threshold.

Requested designer help is part of the CMPL and MFD process, and solo designer analysis is explicitly permitted. The resulting cases therefore do not supply independent blinded review or inter-rater reliability. They also do not separate authorship, design ownership and analysis in ChemTrains. Multiple problems, designs, sessions and publications must not be counted as independent participant replications.

## Actual correspondence with the archived draft (S307)

The compared draft is the complete 36-page PPIG-archived file under parent `M72U8E7C`, attachment `RNAT5UJM` v6491, SHA256 **`43f2493c259b44ea88f219b60ee0e058468070dfb267568c9a000eb2dedd4938`**. It is undated on the manuscript, explicitly says “draft” and asks not to be cited; 1992 comes from the archive context. The published final has the same seven authors and four project accounts, but the correspondence is not merely pagination or copy-editing.

Material continuities and changes are:

- **Stable core:** both editions analyze steps plus guiding knowledge, use the same three problem questions, give a seven-stage cookbook, permit alternative valid routes, report the same four project families and preserve the same main positive, adverse and cost qualifications.
- **Publication framing:** the draft title presents a “structured method” for “writability”; the final title emphasizes aiding design and usually says “ease of programming.” The final removes the draft's opening maintenance/readability trade-off and its separate concluding subsection, while adding received/revised dates and a journal-facing summary. This changes emphasis, not the unit of evidence.
- **Method elaboration:** the final adds a five-step outline before the example; explicit framing/application-step categories and their completeness check; fuller advice for comparing divergent solution routes; a stronger preference for outside analysts; and a clearer split between assumed language-independent background and language-specific material that documentation must cover. The final cookbook drops the draft's explicit checklist item about conflicts among pieces of guiding knowledge or between guidance and users' prior knowledge, although the ChemTrains account still reports expectation conflicts.
- **CMPL reconstruction and timing:** the draft names a suite of 23 problems and says the two sessions lasted about two hours each. The final omits the suite count, says each session lasted about two to three hours and later summarizes five to six hours. It replaces the draft's Roman-numeral knowledge inventory with ten explicit step/guiding-knowledge pairs, changes the initial macro decomposition from three functional parts to four syntactic parts including the result declaration, and warns that the polished order was not the analysts' actual path. Its description of the adopted type semantics is also more concrete about avoiding incremental grammar/parser generation.
- **MFD evidence:** the draft summarizes “several” difficulties and leaves two annotation changes largely unspecified. The final reports an approximately three-hour session, labels five major difficulties and describes the history-scope decision, the removal of editable running-state values, the variable/constant distinction and whole-message-template simplification. This improves traceability without converting the account into a controlled outcome study.
- **ChemTrains reporting:** the comparative structure and user-check claims are substantively stable. The draft calls the comparative effort 30 walkthroughs; the final says more than 30, consistent with its surrounding iterative account but without a new denominator breakdown. The draft's single composite ChemTrains figure becomes separate final Figures 2 and 3. The final still gives no user sample size or prediction-accuracy result.
- **DINO and overall limits:** the alternative-syntax result, two-afternoon scope, documentation effect and warning about competing code-size/knowledge criteria remain materially stable. The final newly calls walkthroughs useful preparation for user testing and more strongly explains why early work on relatively small problems has the highest payoff. It retains the finite-task and judgment limits.

S306 therefore closes the **final-edition correspondence and formal-citation gap** for the walkthrough method and expands the exact MFD/CMPL evidence. The completed [S304 reading](S304-usability-testing-reading-2026-10-15.md) separately closes the reported ChemTrains publication/protocol gap: six volunteers, sequentially changed preparation and interface operation, four assistance fallbacks and before-fallback behavior counts. S304 and S306 remain overlapping reports of the same ChemTrains lineage, not independent datasets, and neither supplies a complete prediction denominator or validated accuracy measure.

## Consequence for the existing Nu role and assistance inventory

The final supports the existing R11/R12 inventory at a narrower and better sourced level. For each prepared role/task, keep separate:

- the **framing decision** (for example, choosing to treat visible text, entity identity and the F# handle as separate obligations) from the **application method** used to edit or reconcile them;
- domain/background knowledge, a language/tool operation, a task plan and an explanation of consequences;
- intended and unexpected valid routes, including ordinary search, manual reconciliation, composition/helpers and counted assistance;
- analyst predictions from actual observed choices, requested help, intermediate behavior and independently checked retained/new behavior.

The final's background-versus-documentation distinction helps locate a proposed cue, but does not establish that the cue is sufficient or that a user who needs it is ineligible. Its framing/application check can expose missing hypotheses in a preparation sheet; it is not a participant score. The CMPL/MFD assistance histories reinforce counting help and total work. The ChemTrains mismatch between predicted knowledge and user behavior reinforces preserving omitted basics, prior expectations and refusal to follow an unexplained technique.

No row in the current role inventory becomes validated by this reading. The practical task, target population, preparation condition, observation reliability and independently correct maintenance outcome remain separate decisions. S304 is now complete and confirms concrete user behavior and assistance levels, while leaving predictive-validity measurement unresolved. Further progress on that claim requires prediction-level records or a new validation design rather than another analyst-authored walkthrough example. Human comparison remains deferred under the existing authority.
