## What the method does

The method examines a hypothetical user's route from a concrete problem to a program. It inventories both proposed steps and the **knowledge needed to select them**: domain concepts, problem-solving plans and language details. Merely listing syntax or showing that a solution exists misses the intended analysis. Nor is the analyst reporting their own mental process; an expert's ease with a step does not establish that the intended user has the necessary knowledge (§§2.1–2.3).

The procedure defines the relevant language features and sample problems, identifies intended solutions, convenes analysts, works through decisions and alternatives, records the knowledge that would guide them, then uses the results to reconsider design or documentation. Problems should include realistic domain demands and should not contain artificial hints that presuppose the proposed language's concepts. Large problems may receive explicitly partial analysis. Intended solutions help scrutinize a design rationale, but the designer can initially withhold them and accept an appropriate alternative route.

The analysis asks where a route is long, where adequate guidance cannot be stated, and where the needed concepts are extensive or unfamiliar. It also examines contradictory guidance, ambiguous language definitions and a mismatch between intended and plausible use. These are qualitative judgments. The draft supplies no calibrated step-weighting scheme, proficiency cutoff, inter-analyst reliability estimate or predictive error rate. Its recommendations do not license exposing successor solutions to research candidates or treating a preferred patch as the only valid outcome.

## Four experience accounts, with their actual units

| Project | Work and useful reported result | Evidence boundary |
| --- | --- | --- |
| CMPL macro language, §§2.2, 3.1 | Four authors unfamiliar with CMPL worked with its designers. Of 23 supplied problems, they examined three over two sessions of about two hours. The walkthrough exposed a poorly motivated typed/typeless distinction, terminology problems and a simpler delimiter-conversion solution than the designers expected. The designers revised the language and accepted the alternative solution. | This is an assisted design session, not four representative novice participants or a randomized usability comparison. The printed reconstruction intentionally omits false starts, mistakes and most discussion. Four elapsed session-hours are not total person-work. Some missing features were expected to emerge in ordinary development anyway. |
| MFD protocol notation, §3.2 | Four authors, none experienced protocol designers, examined two protocol problems with the designer available for requested help. They preferred explicit state over the intended history mechanism, exposed ambiguity about a history condition's scope and prompted simpler message notation. | The language manual existed before implementation. Changed definitions and designer judgments support a concrete design contribution; no measured learning/time advantage or runtime protocol correctness is supplied. The domain-expertise mismatch limits transfer. |
| ChemTrains visual simulation, §3.3 | Three designers each analyzed one of three designs on six initial problems; a later four-problem set retained the preference for the more powerful OPSTrains design but exposed missing timing/synchrony and rotation features. The simpler ZeroTrains design demanded more techniques and problem solving in their analyses. Later iterative design expanded an inventory from 35 to 95 choices over three months. | Analyst and design are confounded in the initial comparison. Thirty walkthroughs are ten problems across three designs, not thirty independent participants or trials. The later single-designer exploration is another process account, not an independently tested effect. |
| DINO parallel language, §3.4 | Two authors compared implicit communication notation with explicit send/receive operations over two afternoons. The implicit form sometimes used less code but needed more guiding techniques. Boundary-element handling emerged as another difficulty; resulting documentation included the knowledge inventory and two worked examples. | This late-stage exercise mainly informed documentation. Code size and predicted difficulty favored different options; implementation trade-offs remained. No comparative learner outcome or general superiority result is reported. |

These are favorable, situated accounts of useful design changes and documentation. They also preserve failures of initial expectations and limits of the method. The authors' judgment that the work repaid its cost is not a measured return-on-investment comparison. They found the thirty ChemTrains walkthroughs burdensome and judged the smaller, earlier CMPL/MFD analyses more practical (§4.2). Preparation, writing and discussion belong in the cost boundary.

## What the reported user check does and does not establish

For ChemTrains, only the preferred OPSTrains design is described as implemented and tested with users. After receiving descriptions of its techniques, users attempted a phase-change simulation with an adjustable burner (§3.3.2). This is not a user comparison among all three designs. The draft says the check broadly supported the walkthrough while exposing three shortcomings: omitted basic knowledge that designers took for granted, explanations too weak to overcome existing expectations, and users unwilling to follow a technique without understanding why it worked.

The source gives no participant denominator, detailed allocation, complete protocol coding, quantified prediction accuracy or independent readiness criterion for that check. Those details cannot be supplied from the title or abstract of unread S304. Likewise, success on the additional four authored problems does not establish representative problem coverage. The paper explicitly warns that finite examples miss important demands and that walkthroughs structure intuition rather than eliminate it (§4.1).

## Consequence for Nu preparation

Use a walkthrough as a **candidate prerequisite and assistance inventory** for a specified role/task. Link each proposed decision to the knowledge or external cue that could support it, its actual source, plausible alternatives and what later observation would confirm or challenge the prediction. Separate knowledge of an operation, a plan for using it and an explanation of its consequences. Available documentation, a successful analyst solution and a supplied sequence of instructions are different evidence from a newcomer independently making the right choice.

For the existing R11 rename/presentation case, changing visible text, changing an entity identity and preserving an event reference pose different decisions. For R12 family propagation, the supported default/correspondence policy does not tell us whether the intended author understands which instance values should change. These are maintainer applications to already specified cases, not new participant findings or completed Nu walkthrough sessions.

The [R07/R20 learning contract](../../nu-learning-and-role-contract-2026-10-11.md) now uses the method at this limited level. A preparation checklist must not become a validated readiness gate merely because its author can explain every step. Keep observed assistance, alternative solutions, unexpected misconceptions and unsuccessful training; assess actual behavior separately. Before adopting a validated prediction/readiness claim, resolve S304's original user protocol and the final-edition correspondence, then separately authorize task/access and human measurement work. No participant, observer, worker or candidate experiment is created here.
