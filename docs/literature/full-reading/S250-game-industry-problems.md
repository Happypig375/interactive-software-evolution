## Corpus, coding and the meaning of a problem

Sections2–3, pp2–6, extend the earlier S251 corpus. Authors iteratively select reports, code negative-section passages using prior categories, discuss classification and revise the dataset. They report category stabilization at about140 reports, continuing to200; some selections deliberately balance years and unsuitable reports are replaced. Initial random selection therefore does not make the final200 a probability sample of games or studios. No complete rejected-report flow or independent coding-reliability statistic is supplied.

The paper reports **927 problem entries in200 game projects,1997–2019**,20 types and four groups: production, people management, feature management and business. A second coding pass creates105 subtypes, described as root causes. These are interpretations of reported accounts; there is no causal intervention or independently observed sequence establishing that each subtype caused its outcome. The sample contains released and mostly profitable games, excludes cancelled failures, depends on voluntary disclosure and often lacks technical detail. Several entries can come from one game, and platform memberships overlap.

This remains useful evidence. Sections4/6 reconstruct ten recurring reported concerns and proposed responses: staffing, working environment, marketing, estimation, clarity/fun/complexity of game design, platform constraints, tool suitability and team alignment. Advice includes prototyping/playtesting, matching tool choices to goals and expertise, accounting for platform limits and coordinating multidisciplinary work. Recommendations synthesize reports and the authors' wider knowledge; they are not ten tested interventions or a general measured return on adopting agile methods.
