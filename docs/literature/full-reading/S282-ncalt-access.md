# S282 — NCAlt alternatives, behavior-tree differencing and bounded usability evidence

Md. Yousuf Hossain and Loutfouz Zaman, *NCAlt: Alternatives and Difference Visualizations for Behavior Trees in Game Development Learning*, *Proceedings of the ACM on Human-Computer Interaction* 6, CHI PLAY, article 245, 1–31 (October 2022). [DOI 10.1145/3549508](https://doi.org/10.1145/3549508). The published article reports receipt in February 2022, revision in June 2022 and acceptance in July 2022.

**Complete published-edition reading, 2026-10-15:** NCAlt gives game-development students explicit behavior-tree alternatives, one-to-many structural differences, simultaneous scene previews and selective node merging. Sixteen paid students rated the complete NCAlt package more usable than ordinary NodeCanvas on two fixed, scaffolded tasks. The positive result is real but bounded: the aggregate SUS difference is 9.9 points, with a strong system-by-order interaction; efficiency, ease and selective merging do not differ significantly in the study-specific ratings; no task time, correctness or learning outcome is measured. A one-person open-ended follow-up shows sustained use, not a comparative effect.

## What is an alternative, and what is preserved

NCAlt modifies NodeCanvas source inside Unity. Its unit of preservation is principally a **behavior-tree document alternative**, not a saved running game world (pp. 5–15).

| Operation | Actual mechanism | Boundary relevant to a later comparison |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Create and edit | Creating an alternative copies the current BT into a separate JSON file. A separate alternative-set JSON stores a unique alternative ID and name. Subsequent node addition, deletion, rewiring or complete replacement in one alternative does not change the others. | This preserves document branches after the copy. It does not by itself preserve heap objects, timers, blackboard values, external effects or a playable scene snapshot. Initial construction and every retained alternative remain part of storage and authoring work. |
| Select and instantiate | A radio control selects the active BT, including during playtesting. A separate action clones a new game object carrying the selected alternative. The paper says only the active combination is compiled when play begins. | Choosing a document, switching the BT used by an existing object and creating another object are different operations. The paper does not specify what happens to ongoing node execution, local variables, timers or effects when an active BT changes. Returning to an earlier alternative is therefore not demonstrated runtime rollback. |
| Compare in the scene | The user selects any number of alternatives against an active reference. NCAlt creates colored and labelled clone objects and opens their trees side by side, so the alternatives execute concurrently in one scene. | The view usefully exposes behavioral differences, but the comparison uses distinct objects. During the main study, clone objects could collide and disrupt one another; the authors later put comparison clones on a non-colliding layer. Simultaneous visibility is not an equivalence oracle or a same-state comparison. |
| Compare tree structure | One active tree is the reference. NCAlt marks added nodes, shows deleted nodes translucently and disabled, marks changed parameters and numbers priority changes. The user can change the reference. | The visualization exposes syntactic and ordering differences. It does not certify that unchanged-looking nodes have equal context or that the resulting behaviors meet a requirement. Large trees introduce pan/zoom and screen-space costs. |
| Selective merge | Selected source nodes are copied to a target. A node absent from the target keeps its source ID; if the “same” node already exists, NCAlt assigns a new ID. A missing parent requires the user either to leave the nodes disconnected or choose another resolution. A positional conflict offers Replace, Place Before or Place After. | The merge carries nodes and order under local rules, not a behavioral postcondition. The paper does not fully define the node-matching predicate, cross-alternative reference repair or semantic conflict detection. New IDs, disconnected nodes and order choices can change identity and behavior and therefore require independent checks. |

The two worked examples are also the later fixed study tasks: three RPG enemy variants and three Pac-Man ghost behaviors. They are demonstrations and assigned examples rather than independent deployments. The open-ended follow-up reveals a further scope ambiguity. Its participant used three final BTs as state-specific behaviors under a finite-state machine, although some were created for different purposes rather than as rival solutions. NCAlt can therefore act as both a version/alternative manager and a container for behavior components selected at runtime. A future design must declare whether alternatives are mutually exclusive candidate solutions, retained history, concurrent comparison objects or composable runtime states.

## Main user study

### Participants, allocation and preparation

The study recruited **16 paid participants, three women**, aged 20–29, from technical undergraduate and graduate game-development or computer-science programs (p. 16). All reported two to seven years of Unity experience (mean 3.4 years) and at least one node-based language/system (mean 1.5 years); several had Unreal Blueprints, Blender, Flow Canvas or other visual-language experience. The paper describes them as moderately skilled students, not professional developers or first-time learners. The a priori calculation targeted only a large within-between interaction effect and selected 16 as sufficient for power .8.

Each participant used both systems. The **2 × 2 mixed design** treats System (ordinary NodeCanvas, `NC`, versus NCAlt) within participants and system Order between participants. Table 1 crosses four groups of four participants over system order and task order: Pac-Man/NC then RPG/NCAlt; RPG/NC then Pac-Man/NCAlt; Pac-Man/NCAlt then RPG/NC; or RPG/NCAlt then Pac-Man/NC. This balances the two game tasks across systems at the sample level, but each participant sees a different task under each system and the analysis does not report Task as a factor.

Both tasks began from supplied Unity projects containing game objects and scenes. Player movement, several ghost variables/functions and the main RPG character were already implemented; the assigned NPCs had empty BTs. Participants built three alternatives from scratch. Under NCAlt they used explicit alternatives; under NC they copied whole trees onto the same canvas. The tasks deliberately offered little creative freedom. A written tutorial preceded each condition, with NCAlt's tutorial additionally covering alternative management, tree/scene differences and selective merging; participants then completed a tutorial warm-up. A full session averaged about two hours and included questionnaires and interview. Three monitors were recorded and the investigator took notes. The article does not give the exact task sheets, tutorial bodies, correctness rubric, assistance/request policy or compensation amount.

### Quantitative and qualitative results

The strongest positive outcome is package-level perceived usability (pp. 18–21):

- Mean SUS is **74.9 (SD 7.47) for NCAlt versus 65.0 (SD 9.0) for NC**, a 9.9-point absolute difference or about **15.2% relative to NC**. The reported System effect is `F(1,14)=40.38`, `p<.0001`, partial eta-squared `.74`. The abstract's “15% higher” is therefore not a 15-point change, a standardized effect or a learning gain.
- Order is also significant, `F(1,14)=14.17`, `p<.01`, partial eta-squared `.50`, as is System × Order, `F(1,14)=9.65`, `p<.01`, partial eta-squared `.40`. In the NC-first group, NC is 72.5 and NCAlt 77.5 and the pairwise difference is not significant. In the NCAlt-first group, NCAlt is 72.25 and later NC is 57.68, with the reported pairwise difference `p<.01`. The aggregate advantage is thus accompanied by asymmetric order/contrast or carryover; it is not a context-free 9.9-point effect.
- In the seven-point author questionnaire, medians favor NCAlt for future use (4 versus 6), multiple BTs (2.5 versus 6), selective merging (3 versus 4), game-scene differences (3 versus 6), tree differences (3 versus 5) and overall (4.5 versus 5.5); efficiency and ease are both 5 under both systems. The reported System differences are significant for future use, multiple alternatives, both diff modes and overall. They are **not significant for efficiency, ease of use or selective merging**. Several Order effects remain, while no questionnaire category has a significant System × Order interaction.
- Appendix A says it gives the exact author questions, but item 7 asks about “swapping nodes,” whereas Table 3 labels the corresponding category “Diffing: BT.” The paper does not reconcile that wording. Table 2 also prints `ns > 0.5`, evidently inconsistent with the surrounding `.05` threshold. Use the tabled categories cautiously and do not treat this instrument as a validated exploration or learning scale.

All participants preferred NCAlt for the assigned tasks, and interviews favor explicit alternatives, switching, organization and both difference views. Four participants said NCAlt made them more willing to try ideas, and several perceived faster testing or debugging. These are useful positive experience reports. The study did **not** compare task time, independently scored behavior, number or quality of explored ideas, retained knowledge or learning. The authors explicitly exclude completion speed because they expected high task variability, and the fixed tasks allowed little creativity.

The qualitative evidence also preserves adverse results. The interview account says participants without prior node-based-system experience were less interested because both systems felt complex, although the participant profile says everyone had at least one node-based language/system; the subgroup definition is unresolved. Some confused selective merge with ordinary copy/paste and could not use it properly; a participant struggled to find where a merge landed. Two participants found both systems insufficient because the node vocabulary did not replace needed scripts. Scene-versus-tree diff preferences changed with tree size and whether all NPCs could fit in the game view. Pac-Man's three 15-node trees required pan/zoom, while seven of eight RPG/NCAlt participants preferred the tree view because its 7-, 10- and 15-node alternatives were easier to inspect than spatially separated scene objects.

The main study exposed implementation defects that the authors report repairing after the evaluation: missing deletion caused a mistaken branch to be renamed “deleted alt”; comparison clones collided; merge highlights were hard to see; and opening multiple trees required inconvenient inspector navigation. The reported changes add deletion buttons, brighter merge highlights, non-colliding clone layers and relocated controls. Only the deletion control is explicitly said to have been added before the follow-up, so the exact follow-up build correspondence for the other fixes is unresolved; at minimum, it does not use exactly the main-study interface.

## One-person open-ended follow-up

One convenience-sampled participant from the main study used the revised NCAlt over two weeks on a self-chosen two-person shooting game, with only an AI-character requirement (pp. 23–26). This is an overlapping participant, not a new comparative sample. Activity logs show use on nine days for a mean of about 68 minutes per day; five BT alternatives were created and two deleted. The participant used scene diff 12 times, tree diff 10 times and selective merge five times, and positively described the combination of finite-state machines and BT alternatives.

This is valuable evidence that the features were used outside the fixed lab tasks. It is not evidence of a productivity or learning effect. Daily node creation increased from about eight during the first four days to about 30 later, but project phase, task content and growing familiarity all change together. There is no NodeCanvas comparator, correctness assessment, matched project, independent observation of final quality or total-work baseline. The single participant and post-study tool changes preclude treating the follow-up as replication of the main SUS result.

## Relationship to S283 NCCollab

NCAlt and [S283 NCCollab](S283-nccollab-collaboration-history.md) share both authors, modified NodeCanvas infrastructure and a single integrated application; NCAlt alternatives are available inside collaborative activities (p. 4). That is shared implementation and author lineage. The experiments remain distinct in the published accounts:

- S282 uses 16 paid students individually on Pac-Man and RPG alternative tasks, comparing NCAlt with ordinary NodeCanvas.
- S283 reports 12 participants arranged in six pairs on Slime, TurtleShell and Beholder collaboration tasks, comparing solitary NodeCanvas with synchronous/asynchronous NCCollab packages.
- S282 explicitly says the NCCollab study **did not include alternatives** (p. 28), while S283 explicitly delegates alternatives evaluation to this separate publication.

Neither article supplies participant identities that establish overlap or disjointness. Do not combine their participant denominators, treat them as independent replications of one intervention, or transfer NCCollab's completion-time result to NCAlt. Their common code base also means the two tools are not independent implementations.

## Total-work and inference boundary

The comparison gives NCAlt credit for its realistic feature bundle and gives NodeCanvas credit for ordinary whole-tree duplication on one canvas. NodeCanvas was not designed for explicit alternatives, so the study compares a purpose-built package with a credible but intentionally less organized ordinary workflow. It does not isolate the effect of JSON separation, alternative selection, tree diff, scene diff or merging. Tutorials, warm-up, supplied scripts/scenes, three-monitor setup and feature-specific authoring work are part of the evaluated package.

Unmeasured costs include modifying and maintaining NodeCanvas source, building alternative and identity storage, creating/retaining alternatives, resolving merge placement and IDs, compiling and verifying behaviors, repairing comparison collisions, integrating missing node types or scripts, training/help, and maintaining the tool across NodeCanvas/Unity changes. The paper gives neither construction effort nor a reuse count over which to amortize it. A favorable SUS score does not show reduced total development time, correct behavior after a merge, preserved runtime state or improved student learning.

Statistically, 16 repeated participants are the unit for the main study, not 32 independent system observations or six alternative trees per participant. The two game tasks and three alternatives are repeated work within people. Counterbalancing addresses systematic order/task assignment but does not remove the reported order interaction or supply an unreported task model. The study is powered for an anticipated large effect and has no raw response/log release here for reanalysis. Preserve the favorable effect sizes and null categories as reported rather than interpreting nonsignificance as equivalence.

## Consequence for the Nu research design and remaining gaps

S282 closes the primary-body, figures/tables, appendix and formal-method access gap. It changes an alternative-comparison design in five ways:

1. Define whether an alternative is a saved program/document, a branch that may later merge, a concurrent comparison object or a runtime-selectable component. Do not treat those as one state-preservation claim.
2. Specify creation, active selection, scene instantiation and combination separately. For live switching, bind what happens to entity identity, local values, timers, events, effects and failed/in-progress actions.
3. Give merges a stable identity/matching policy, reference and ordering rules, explicit unresolved states, and independent behavioral checks. A visible tree diff is diagnostic evidence rather than a semantic oracle.
4. Credit ordinary duplication, composition, conditional execution and manual comparison as valid alternatives, with their clutter and reconciliation costs visible. Count the purpose-built tool, tutorial, integration and repair work on the NCAlt side.
5. Measure the decision that matters. SUS and preference support usability; correctness, useful exploration, learning, time, total person-work, retention and transfer require their own predeclared outcomes. Preserve order/carryover and all attempted alternatives rather than selecting only the final favorite.

Remaining gaps are exact NodeCanvas/Unity versions, source code and build correspondence; full task/tutorial materials; participant-level SUS and questionnaire responses; raw videos, notes and activity logs; assistance and compensation details; statistical scripts; final behavior/correctness criteria; the node-matching/diff implementation; and the runtime-state semantics of switching an active BT. No supplementary artifact is identified in the article, and none was acquired in this reading. The historical repository screening found no NCAlt-named public repository, but that is not proof that code or data are unavailable everywhere.
