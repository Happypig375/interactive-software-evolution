## What the method establishes

This is a secondary classification of a selected earlier review, **not 48 new interventions or a fresh search of the 2026 field**. It takes 37 case-study and eleven experiment labels from [S268](S268-industry-scale-game-software-review.md), whose scope is industry-scale game software and whose stated search range is 2009–2021. The choice of a commercial game/engine does not establish professional participants, a production intervention or current field prevalence. The paper itself acknowledges alternative methodological taxonomies and that game-specific outlets may be missed (pp. 5–7, 16).

For case studies, the operational rule requires an identifiable case, real context, multiple collection methods and a contemporary phenomenon, with no active long-term change role for the researcher. The latter separates its chosen case-study definition from action research; the authors acknowledge that other disciplines permit participatory or retrospective cases. Counting occurrences of “interview” is assessed as a screening indicator, not the substantive definition. The work usefully distinguishes a research method from an instrument such as a questionnaire (pp. 1, 5–6).

For controlled experiments, **§4.3 separates manipulation, control and random treatment allocation (QEX1–3) from quality/reporting attributes (QEX4–7)**: hypotheses/threats, statistics, replication materials and causal language. That distinction matters. A replication package enables scrutiny; its presence alone does not establish an appropriate comparator, unbiased allocation, causal identification or successful independent reproduction. Causal words are what QEX7 codes, not verified causation.

Two authors independently code a pilot of five cases/five experiments selected in the earlier review’s order and resolve differences. The remaining 32 cases/six experiments are randomly split between them: **19 papers per coder**, not an independently double-coded remainder. Flagged doubts receive paired consensus discussion. The described procedure does not supply agreement statistics, all initial judgments or the source-located answer notes. Authors overlap with S268, and several authors also wrote the two praised experimental exemplars; this makes the stated validation scope relevant, without invalidating those studies (pp. 7, 15–16).

## Results worth retaining, with their units

The authors report one case study, four action-research studies, five archival analyses and 27 small evaluations. Their examples explain why mining a real game’s old logs, testing a prototype with game assets and observing a current development project answer different questions. The sole prose exemplar is Engström/Ostblad’s text-to-speech production study; its described sources include field notes, project documents, communication and player/store data. Playtesting collaborations and Gamespect illustrate researcher participation in change; historical telemetry illustrates archival evidence (pp. 7, 9–10).

The paper explicitly says action research, archival research and small evaluations are **not inherently inferior** (p. 13). Their relevance depends on the question and claim. Its practical guidance—state the unit/context, separate actual intervention from observation, document controls and offer usable materials—can improve a future Nu study without discarding engineering demonstrations or situated practice accounts. Playtesting concerns player experience; it is not automatically a test of developer maintenance benefit.

Neither is a population estimate of valid GSE experiments or a measure of whether those interventions helped.
